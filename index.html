        About Delta911 · problem, research, how it works, mock data

        Delta911: AI call triage for 911 surges that clusters duplicate reports and surfaces only new, life-safety information

        Delta911 is a dispatcher console for 911 call centers (PSAPs) during a call-volume spike after a major incident: a fire, shooting, stabbing, riot or crash. Every incoming call is transcribed, its facts are extracted, and the facts are diffed against what the incident already knows. A call that only repeats known facts is answered by the AI with a dispatcher-approved message and stays on the line, in place. A call that adds a new fact (someone trapped, a second victim, a suspect's direction) is flagged to a human immediately, and the new fact is added to the incident card for responders.

        Built for the Plug and Play × PMAI Hackathon, Challenge 3: 911 call surges. All calls in this prototype are scripted mock data.

        Current status: the problem

        • Duplicate reports swamp the lines. Dozens to hundreds of bystanders report the same obvious event. Lahaina, 2023: 4,523 calls in one day against an average of 360, with dispatch 51% short-staffed. Sedgwick County, KS: 168 calls in under 10 minutes during a mall shooting; the director said it would take 168 dispatchers to avoid holds.
        • Critical calls get buried. At Apalachee High School (2024), callers heard a "high call volume" recording and one waited more than 10 minutes. The caller trapped on the 3rd floor can be #75 in line behind 74 people describing the same smoke.
        • New facts get lost. "There's a second victim" or "he ran toward Herald Square" changes the response, but it is spoken into the same queue as everyone else.
        • Staffing is thin. In a 2022 IAED/NASNA survey of 774 centers, about a third reported vacancy rates of 30% or more. In a 2023 NENA/Carbyne survey, 74% of respondents reported burnout.

        Delta911 extends the one proven pattern (Carbyne's "same incident nearby" triage) from crashes to mass-casualty surges. It adds two things no product clearly offers: novelty detection (diffing each call against known facts) and human-confirmed split and merge for different incidents at the same corner.

        Hero feature 1: Delta Detection

        Each incident keeps a card of known facts. Every call's transcript is turned into facts (incident type, location, people at risk, injuries, suspect details) and compared with the card:

        • No new facts: the call is counted as a confirmation of the incident's size. The AI answers with the broadcast the dispatcher approved, and the caller keeps their place in line and can speak up at any time.
        • New facts: the call is flagged red for a dispatcher with a one-line summary, and the fact is added to the card ("+ Person trapped, 3rd floor, from call #75") so responders get it immediately.

        This replaces "voice stress" scoring. A National Institute of Justice field test found voice stress analysis about as accurate as chance, and it risks bias against accents and non-native speakers. A calm caller can be in mortal danger. What the caller says is a fairer and more reliable signal than how they sound.

        Hero feature 2: one-tap split and merge

        Geography alone would merge a stabbing reported at 5th Ave & 34th St into the fire at the same corner. Delta911 treats the incident type as a hard boundary: it flags "type mismatch, suggest split" and waits for a dispatcher. One tap creates a second incident with its own card, its own broadcast and its own priority. When unsure, the system keeps incidents apart, because a wrong merge hides a victim and a wrong split only costs a second look.

        Safety rules built into the design

        • The AI never hangs up a 911 call. Clustered callers stay on the line and keep their place, and hear: "If you are inside, hurt, or have new information, say so now." Callers who hang up get an automatic text and go into a callback queue, so no call is dropped.
        • Dispatcher-confirmed broadcasts only. The message a caller hears is approved by a human, and kept generic (no suspect location or tactical details).
        • The AI flags; the dispatcher chooses. The phone system still holds calls in arrival order, which some state rules require. Delta911 sorts the dispatcher's view and flags calls, and a human decides who to pick up.
        • Priority comes from content only, never from neighborhood or area. A call from a quiet block counts the same as one from Midtown.
        • Everything is reversible. Every clustering decision is in the audit log with one-tap Undo ("pull out of cluster").

        Two scores: incident severity and call priority

        Delta911 separates two questions that are usually mixed together. Incident severity answers "how big could this get, and how many units does it need?" Call priority answers "which caller should a dispatcher pick up next?"

        • Incident severity (potential impact) = incident type + people at risk + whether it can spread. Structure fire 55, stabbing 60, minor crash 10. People at risk: a 6-story residential building (~40 people) +25, a victim and bystanders +5, two uninjured drivers +5. Spreading: fire +12, armed suspect at large +15, crash 0. Result: building fire 92, stabbing 80, fender bender 15. A full building on fire outranks a single stabbing, and both far outrank a crash.
        • Call priority = the severity of the incident the call belongs to + new life-safety facts in this call: person trapped +30, person who can't get out alone +20, suspect details +15, injured victim +10 (max 100). Calls that add nothing new score 10 and are clustered. Red is 80 or more, amber 50 or more, green below 50.
        • Duplicate volume is a size signal. 87 people reporting the same fire means it is large and visible. The incident card shows the count, which supports the severity estimate; it is shown, not added to the score, so a loud event can't push out a quiet one.
        • Impact = people at risk, never neighborhood. Scale is the estimated number of people in danger (from building type, floors, reported victims), never the area's income, demographics or address.
        • Severity is designed to map onto the standardized dispatch levels that centers already use (for example, determinant levels in the Fire and Medical Priority Dispatch Systems), so it recommends a response level in a format dispatchers already know.

        Why the demo shows only about 120 calls

        New York's 911 system already handles millions of calls a year, so a normal night is never quiet. The demo shows one Midtown sector's console, with normal citywide calls (a cardiac arrest in Queens, a fall in Staten Island, noise and lockout calls) still arriving underneath the surge. That is the real problem: a surge lands on top of normal load, so call-takers are already busy when the duplicates hit. Delta911 keeps the quiet emergencies elsewhere (the CPR call in Queens) at the top of the queue instead of letting them get buried under 100 reports of a fire everyone can see. The approach scales with volume: the more duplicates, the more calls clustering saves.

        Regional compliance profile

        911 rules differ by state. Each deployment loads a compliance profile for its PSAP (shown in the header). Where an AI-answered held line is permitted, duplicates hear the approved broadcast automatically. Where a human must answer every call, the AI only groups the duplicates and prepares the approved reply, and a dispatcher answers them with one tap. In every profile, the AI never hangs up and never closes a call on its own.

        Mock data and the real feeds they stand in for

        • Call stream: scripted calls with arrival times, caller numbers and transcripts. In production: NG911 SIP/i3 call delivery (the FCC's 2024 NG911 order requires carriers to deliver IP calls on request) plus device location from RapidSOS-style location data.
        • Speech-to-text: mock transcripts. In production: streaming ASR on the first 10–15 seconds of each call, with translation.
        • Fact extraction: mock extracted facts. In production: an LLM extractor with a fixed fact schema (type, location, people at risk, injuries, hazards, suspect), returning only facts it can quote from the transcript.
        • Clustering: location within a radius, plus the same incident type, plus a time window. Type mismatch blocks an automatic merge.
        • Map: a simplified Midtown grid around 5th Ave and 34th St.

        Why mock it: the demo must be repeatable and must never touch real 911 audio. Each mock record has the same shape as the production record, so wiring in live feeds means writing adapters, not redesigning.

        Try the demo (about 60 seconds)

        1. Press Play. The first fire report arrives and duplicates start to pile up.
        2. When asked, Confirm & broadcast the fire. Waiting duplicates collapse into one cluster that is answered by the AI, with the line held.
        3. A stabbing is reported at the same corner. Tap Split to create a second incident.
        4. Watch the call "I'm on the third floor and I can't breathe", jump out of the noise with a red NEW fact.
        5. Check the header: every call answered, only a handful needed a human, and 0 were dropped.

        Impact we would measure in a pilot

        • Time from a new life-safety fact being spoken to a dispatcher hearing it.
        • Share of surge calls that need a human versus calls answered with line held.
        • Wrong merges (target: 0) and wrong splits, reviewed through the audit log.
        • Abandoned calls that received a callback.
